Ask a team what training they need and you usually get a list of technologies.
Ask them six months later what changed, and the honest answer is often "not
much". The course was fine. It just did not connect to anything before or after
it.

That is the problem our catalogue is built around. Instead of a pile of
unrelated courses, we run **one learning path with four stages**. Every course
sits at a stage, and every stage assumes the one before it.

## Stage 1 — Foundation

The ground every developer stands on: servers, git, containers, modern PHP.

This is the stage teams are most tempted to skip, and the one that costs them
later. A developer who is uneasy on the command line, who treats git as a
mysterious ritual, or who has never built a container image will hit a ceiling
no framework course can lift. Foundation is unglamorous and it pays for itself
faster than anything else we teach.

## Stage 2 — Practitioner

Ship real products the production-quality way: web, mobile, APIs, and
AI-augmented work.

Here the questions change from "how does this work" to "how do we ship this
without regretting it". Laravel and Livewire, Flutter, API design and security,
and working alongside AI tooling rather than around it. The output of this
stage is software other people depend on.

## Stage 3 — Professional

Run serious systems: central identity, observability, and agent
infrastructure.

Shipping is not the same as operating. This stage is about the systems that
only reveal themselves once real users arrive — single sign-on and access
control, knowing what your application is doing in production, and the
infrastructure that supports AI agents in a real environment.

## Stage 4 — Architect

Make design decisions you can defend — with and without AI at your side.

The last stage is about judgement. Not which framework, but which trade-off,
and being able to explain the choice to a room that will live with it for
years.

## Finding your stage

A useful test: **the stage you belong in is the one where the material is
slightly uncomfortable.** If a syllabus reads as obvious, go up. If it reads as
alien, go down one and come back. Teams often split across two stages, and that
is fine — people learn faster next to peers at the same level than in a room
pitched at an average nobody occupies.
